Dutch Reformed Church

Stand where you can see both the clock and one side of the Dutch Reformed Church. If you came in from one of the surrounding streets, think back to the moment you first saw the tower. That view was built into the plan of Aberdeen. The church occupies an eight-sided piece of ground. Four streets lead towards it. Long before anyone put a clock up there, the people laying out the village had given this corner of the Karoo a centre.

The congregation began in eighteen fifty-five. For farming families in this district, attending church in Graaff-Reinet meant a journey. When they established a congregation on the farm Brakkefontein, they adapted the farmhouse for worship. Picture the difference in scale. A room in a house could give neighbours somewhere to pray together. A purpose-built church could bring families from across the district under one roof. The foundation stone for that church was laid in eighteen fifty-nine.

Here is something you could walk around the building and easily miss. Part of the earlier church is still inside the shape you see today. Imagine looking down on the roof from above. The main body and its two side arms make a cross. Architects call the arms the transept. When the church was enlarged, the earlier building became part of that crosspiece. Someone standing there today can be in the later church and in the footprint of its predecessor at the same time.

Now look up from the arms to the clock tower. It belonged to the expansion of nineteen oh seven, roughly half a century after worship began in the farmhouse. At about fifty metres, the tower has a different audience from the entrance below. A doorway addresses the person about to step inside. A clock face that high addresses a whole street. Turn your back to it for a moment and look along the approach. Think how far down that street someone could stand and still read the hour.

Before you leave, pick out one of the much smaller towers on the arms of the church. Follow its outline and then let your eye climb the main tower again. Those repeated upright shapes help knit the old and new parts together. Down at pavement level, the shape of the grounds does something similar for the town. Walk a little way round the corner and the building presents another face to another street. You have only moved a few paces, but you can see why a photograph taken from one direction tells only part of the story. At the next stop, the town clock gives way to a very different silhouette. Look out for the little beasts on the courthouse roof.
