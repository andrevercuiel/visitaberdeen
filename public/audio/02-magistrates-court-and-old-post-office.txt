Magistrate’s Court & Old Post Office

From the pavement on Grey Street, give yourself a small challenge. See if you can find a dragon before you find the front door. The creatures are up on the tiled roof of the old post office and magistrate’s court. They are made of terracotta, a clay shaped and fired until it hardens. Against a bright sky you may see the outline before you see the colour. Once you have found one, come back down to the street and look at the building again. How did a place for collecting letters and hearing cases acquire a roof like this?

The Cape Department of Public Works built this complex in eighteen ninety-eight. Aberdeen already had a magistrate’s court by eighteen eighty. The date of this building marks a new home for public services that people in the district already needed. The complex brought court business, postal business and government offices together. Picture two people arriving on the same morning. One has come for a letter from family far away. The other has been summoned to a hearing. They approach the same public building with very different things on their minds.

Start at the base of the tower. Follow the edges upwards as far as you can. Its eight-sided shape gives it a succession of narrow faces. A flat wall facing you might be evenly lit, while the next face of the tower is already in shade. Higher up, the tower narrows. Above it is a spire, then a rounded, bulb-like detail and a flagpole. From a distance those changes merge into one outline. From here you can take them apart, one by one.

Now follow the edge of the red roof away from the tower. Near the eaves, the roof flares out. Underneath, the masonry looks weighty and firmly planted. Above it, the dragons have narrow shapes with daylight around them. That change in scale is part of the pleasure of this building. The stonework can be appreciated by someone standing at the door. A decoration meant to be seen from across Grey Street needs an outline clear enough to survive the distance.

Heritage writers use several style names for the complex, among them Flemish Revival. You need no catalogue of European styles to enjoy what the builders achieved here. Stand a little to one side and see which part disappears first. The face of the tower changes, a stretch of roof comes into view, and a dragon may vanish against the tiles. That short walk gives a better sense of the building than a straight-on photograph. Please keep to the public pavement and leave entrances clear for people with business here. Our next stop takes that eye for detail to a much smaller stone church.
