Wesleyan Methodist Church

At the Wesleyan Methodist Church, begin with the side wall. The easiest detail to overlook here is the piece of wall that sticks out. It has a name, a buttress. Seen straight on, it might look like a stripe of stone. Seen from a little to one side, you can make out its depth. Follow the line of the wall past the windows and look for the next one. On a building with a simple rectangular plan, these projections give the long side a rhythm you can almost count as you walk.

This church dates from the early eighteen eighties. The regional heritage guide places the laying of its foundation stone in eighteen eighty-two and its consecration the following year. Those occasions marked different things. A foundation stone recorded a commitment to build. Consecration marked the setting apart of a finished place for worship. Between those moments came work that seldom makes it onto a commemorative stone. Someone had to set out the walls, bring the materials, prepare the openings and give a roof its support.

Look at one of the arched windows. Begin where its straight sides start to curve, then follow the edge upwards. Making an opening in a stone wall poses a practical question. What holds the masonry above the gap? In a masonry arch, shaped pieces bear against one another and carry the load towards the sides. A buttress adds depth where a wall needs support. Those are useful ideas to keep in mind even when you cannot see the inner construction. They give a reason to look closely at the relationship between the solid stretches of wall and the openings for light.

Now turn to the bell tower. At its top the material changes. The belfry has delicate cast-iron openwork. Look for the sky showing through it. Against the stone below, that small patch of open sky makes the top seem remarkably light. To make cast iron, molten metal is poured into a mould. Curves and repeated shapes can be made together. The eye reads them as something like lace, even though the material is metal. If the light is behind the church, this may be the best detail of all to see in silhouette.

Imagine standing in a neighbouring street when a bell sounded here. People who could see little of the church could still share in the timetable of its congregation. Now bring your attention back to the modest length of that side wall. The rectangular outline, the regular windows and that unexpected crown of iron give the Wesleyan church a character of its own. Carry the shape of one of its windows in your mind. At All Saints, you will have another chance to look at how a congregation used an arch to make a place for worship.
