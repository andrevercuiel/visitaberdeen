Pagel House

At number seventeen Stockenstrom Street, look for the veranda before you look for the name of the house. The veranda wraps around the building. Follow it with your eye to the point where it turns the corner. That turn makes a difference to anyone living here. A person sitting outside can move from one side to another as the sun travels across the sky. An ordinary route from door to garden becomes a sheltered place to spend part of the day.

The house dates from eighteen ninety-seven. Its first name was Claremont House, and its early owner, R. C. Logie Junior, traded in ostrich feathers. Locally, houses of this kind are sometimes called ostrich villas. A fashionable feather worn far from the Karoo might have passed through the hands of someone who lived on this street. When you look at the breadth of that veranda and the care given to its details, you are seeing one way the feather trade left a mark on the town.

One feature described by the guest house is a sheltered sitting nook with stained glass. Think how different that is from a plain sheet of clear glass. Clear glass leaves the garden as the principal thing to look at. Coloured glass makes the opening itself part of the decoration. As the angle of the sun changes, so does the light reaching the seat. Indoors, the property description records painted pressed ceilings and yellowwood flooring in several rooms. A pressed ceiling gets its pattern from a design impressed into sheet material. Details like these can be appreciated without going inside a private guest room.

How, then, did Claremont House become Pagel House? The name came through a later owner, Frank Wilke. He kept a private zoo near Aberdeen and admired the circus performer Wilhelm Pagel. Pagel was known both as a strongman and as a trainer of large animals. His travelling circus was familiar to South African audiences for decades. After his death, Wilke arranged with his widow to acquire marble plaques bearing the Pagel name. It is this connection that explains the name visitors encounter here today.

Think of the house as keeping two quite different records. Its plan and fittings tell you about a family home built in a prosperous trading town. The plaques tell you about a later owner, his admiration for a performer and the journeys an object can make after its first owner dies. From the pavement, look back once more at the corner of the veranda. On a second visit you might remember the circus first. The shaded seat is a reminder that people also came here simply to sit down at the end of the day.
