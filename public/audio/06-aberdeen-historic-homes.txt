Aberdeen’s Historic Homes

For this stop, find two neighbouring homes that you can see from the public pavement. Choose a modest one as well as a house that catches your eye straight away. Begin at the front gate of each, and imagine the short walk from the street to the door. Is the door close enough for someone on the stoep to greet a passer-by? Is there a garden between the street and the veranda? Before you have named a single architectural style, you have already noticed something important about how these houses work.

Aberdeen offers an unusual opportunity to make that comparison. A survey by the Vernacular Architecture Society of South Africa recorded more than two hundred houses in the town dating from before eighteen ninety. That is a remarkable number of ordinary dwellings to set beside the grand villas most often photographed. A small frontage deserves a careful look. The spacing between a door and two windows may give a house much of its character. Try covering the roofline with the edge of your hand for a moment. What still distinguishes one front from the other?

Now uncover the roof and look along its edge. A gable is the end of a wall that rises up against the roof. A veranda has a different job. It carries shelter out beyond the front wall and makes a usable strip of shade. Some Aberdeen houses have intricate trim along the veranda. You may hear the Afrikaans expression broekie lace used for this kind of ornament. Instead of trying to identify the material from a distance, look at its shadow. A plain board makes a dark band. Pierced decoration lets through small shapes of light, and the pattern can be easier to see on the wall than under the roof.

For examples to put on a walking map, the architectural survey lists De Denne in Darling Street, dated eighteen ninety-four, and Villeria in Grey Street, dated eighteen ninety-nine. Carmen Villa in Porter Street dates from eighteen ninety-eight. The dates are close, yet a date alone cannot tell you how a family used a house. Look for a veranda that turns a corner or a window carefully positioned towards a garden. Those choices can say as much about daily life as a date worked into plaster.

Finish where the resident meets the street, at the boundary. A low wall, a gate or a length of decorative wirework can be the best piece of craftsmanship visible from the pavement. Choose one detail you could sketch from memory. Leave the gate as you found it and keep photographs directed away from open windows. These homes have survived long enough for strangers to admire them because generations of people have continued to live in them.
