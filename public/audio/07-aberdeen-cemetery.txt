Aberdeen Cemetery

At Aberdeen Cemetery, begin with a distinction that matters when you walk among the stones. A grave marks a burial. A memorial may name someone whose body lies elsewhere. The two can look very much alike, particularly when lettering has weathered. Start by reading the words on one stone from the path. Does it say that someone lies here, or does it invite you to remember them? That small difference changes what you can safely say about the person it names.

There is a particular reason to ask the question here. The municipal history records the burial of Commandant Carel van Heerden, who died while trying to obtain horses for the Boer forces. It also records a memorial to twenty-five British soldiers who died in the district. One record takes us to a named individual and a particular errand. The other gathers a number of deaths into a public act of remembrance. Hearing the two together tells us something about the war in Aberdeen without requiring us to assume that every soldier commemorated here is buried under that monument.

The South African War lasted from eighteen ninety-nine until nineteen oh two. Aberdeen lay in the Cape Colony. Some local residents joined the Boer forces even though the colonial authorities regarded them as British subjects. For families here, the divisions could run through familiar streets and between people who knew one another. The reference to horses brings the scale of that conflict down to something quite tangible. A mounted group needed fresh horses to move. What might sound like a small errand in a larger war was a task on which a man could lose his life.

There is also a Jewish burial ground beside the Christian cemetery. Its presence gives another starting point for thinking about the town. Along the route you have seen Christian congregations represented by their churches. A burial ground can preserve evidence of another community even when a visitor has seen little trace of its daily life on the main street. If you can read an inscription from the path, take the time to read the whole line. A family relationship or a place of birth can reveal a connection that a surname alone would never suggest.

Before moving on, let your attention settle on an ordinary family stone. Its maker had limited space. Someone had to decide which names, relationships or few words should be cut into it. Weather can make parts of that choice difficult to read. Leave faint lettering as you find it and give anyone visiting a grave room to be alone. The next stop follows one name associated with this burial ground back to a farm, a water supply and the beginnings of the town laid out around us.
