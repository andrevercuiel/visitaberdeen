Jan Vorster & Brakkefontein

Our final stop begins with a name you may encounter at the cemetery, Jan Vorster. Local heritage accounts identify this as his resting place. They also identify him as the owner who sold the farm Brakkefontein to the Dutch Reformed Church. It is easy to shorten that story to a sentence about a town being founded. To see what it meant on the ground, consider three things you have already passed on the route. A place to gather. A street to reach it. And water that could reach a household.

The history of this landscape reaches far beyond a farm boundary. Stone Age material has been recorded in the wider district. Pastoral communities knew where animals could graze and where water could be found. Late seventeenth-century accounts describe a Cape expedition trading for livestock with the Inqua under their leader Heykon. That encounter belongs to a landscape people already knew and used. European stock farming expanded into the region later. By the time a sale of land appeared in church records, generations of people had moved through the Camdeboo for reasons of their own.

In eighteen fifty-five a new Dutch Reformed congregation was established. The farmhouse on Brakkefontein was adapted for worship. In eighteen fifty-seven, plots were auctioned. Imagine the different questions facing the congregation and a prospective householder. The congregation needed a place accessible to families coming in from farms. A household choosing a plot would want to know how to reach the market, where animals could stand, and whether there would be water for a garden.

In eighteen fifty-eight Henry Marriott drew up a town plan. Picture a sheet of squared paper. Most of the streets follow a grid, but in the centre is an eight-sided plot reserved for the church. Four principal streets approach its shorter sides. The plan also provided a market square and an outspan square. To outspan meant to unhitch the animals from a wagon. A market needed space for trade. Travellers needed somewhere for the team that had brought their goods into town. Those two needs took up real space on a map.

Water followed another set of lines. The architectural survey records that about two thirds of the plots were served by water furrows. The Afrikaans word leiwater describes water led along a channel. Think of the difference between seeing water somewhere in a valley and having a channel that can carry it to the edge of your plot. That practical distinction shaped what a family could do with its land.

The settlement took the name Aberdeen from the Scottish birthplace of the Reverend Andrew Murray Senior. When you next see the church tower, look down the street beneath it. The tower came later. The direction of the street, the space around the church and the old routes for water take you closer to the decisions that first gave the town its shape. Thank you for walking through Aberdeen with us.
